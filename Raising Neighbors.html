Raising Neighbors

For parents, grandparents, teachers and guardians: simple ways kids of every age can help take hunger off the table for neighbors across Southeast Michigan, and how to talk with them about it.

Get involved

Ways to help, by age

Every age can help. Pick your child's age to see ideas that fit how they think and grow.

4–7

Small, hands-on things done together, with lots of thanks for what they did.

  • Pick out a box of cereal together for the Children's Hospital Cereal Drive.
  • On Stamp Out Hunger day in May, help fill a bag of food and set it by the mailbox for your letter carrier.
  • Choose one item for a food drive and carry it to the bin.
  • Color pictures or make cards for volunteers.
8–11

Group projects that keep giving private and celebrate the whole class.

  • Run a virtual food drive team page with an adult, for a class, a birthday or a club.
  • Join a KHK Virtual Classroom session with their class or group.
  • Learn about growing food at a Detroit Food Zoo youth nutrition workshop.
  • From age 10, sort and pack food at the Livingston Distribution Center in Howell, alongside an adult.
12–13

A real say in what they do, and a little time to reflect afterward.

  • Lead a drive at school, on a team or in a club, with an adult's help.
  • Bring their school into the Hunger Heroes School Challenge.
  • Make a flyer reminding neighbors about Stamp Out Hunger day.
  • Volunteer at the Livingston Distribution Center in Howell.
14+

Shifts they choose, alongside experienced volunteers.

  • Sort and pack food at the Taylor South Distribution Center (8th grade and up).
  • Stock shelves, bag groceries or fill orders at Fresh! by Gleaners in Warren, with a parent or guardian.
  • Help lead their school's Hunger Heroes effort.
  • Organize a group shift for friends or a team, and log service hours if their school asks.

Children's Hospital Cereal Drive

Gleaners and Children's Hospital of Michigan collect cereal for kids heading into summer, when school meals stop. Families drop off boxes at kickoff and collection events, and groups can start a virtual cereal drive. More than 14.8 million servings raised.

Letter carriers collect food from mailboxes on their routes. Kids can help pick the food, fill the bag and set it out. Since 2010, more than 15.2 million pounds of food have come through post office drives.

At home

Talking with kids, by age

You don't need a script, and "I don't know, let's find out" is a good answer. These ideas follow guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Short and two-way.Ask what they've heard before you explain.
Answer the worry first.Young kids often want to know they're okay before anything else.
Thank the action."Thanks for picking out the beans with me" works better than "you're such a helper."
Keep it private.Anyone can need help, and you can't tell by looking.

Feelings and fairness

"Some families near us don't always have enough groceries. Gleaners helps them get food, and we can help Gleaners."

"Why don't they have enough food?"

"Sometimes a grown-up's job changes, or groceries cost more than they can pay. Nobody did anything wrong."

"Do we have enough food?"

Answer calmly and briefly with what's true for your family. "Making sure there's food is a grown-up job, and we've got it" is often all they need.

Easy to skip: sad photos, "finish your plate" guilt, and prizes for helping.

Privacy and kindness

"What have you noticed or heard about food drives at school?"

"Is anyone in my class hungry?"

"Maybe. Lots of families get help at some point, and you can't tell by looking. It's their private business, so we don't guess and we don't point anyone out."

"If we can't fix it, why bother?"

"One gift doesn't fix everything. It does put real food on real shelves, and lots of gifts add up."

Easy to skip: tallies of who brought what, and "you're lucky" comparisons.

Their own ideas

"I'd like your take. Is there anything you'd want to do, like volunteer together or run a drive? Or nothing. Your call."

"Why does this happen?"

"A lot of everyday things. Prices go up, a job changes, someone gets sick. It can happen to a lot of families. What do you think?"

"Do I have to?"

"No. If you're in, great. If not, that's fine."

Easy to skip: making it required, posting their photo without asking, and lectures.

For teachers

Fun and fair for every student

School drives are many kids' first connection to Gleaners. A few habits keep them fun and fair.

  1. Start with a lesson. Book a KHK Virtual Classroom session so students know why the drive matters.
  2. Celebrate the whole class or school. Count by class, never by student, and keep any prize for everyone.
  3. Make giving private. Use a bin anyone can drop into, and let families give online.
  4. Count food and dollars the same. Some families can give a can, some a dollar, and some neither. All of that is fine.
  5. Ask what's needed. Gleaners can share its most-needed list.
  6. Close the loop. Share the class total and give students a minute to say what they noticed.

For grandparents

Your stories matter

You don't need a lesson plan. If you have a story about a time food was tight, or a time you helped someone or someone helped you, tell it. Family stories help kids understand where they come from and what the family values. Keep it short and let them ask questions. Then do one small thing together.

  • Make a gift in a grandchild's honor and tell them what it provides.
  • Start a family team page for the Cereal Drive.
  • Fill a Stamp Out Hunger bag together in May.
  • Volunteer together in Howell once they're 10.

Notes for the Gleaners team: the research behind this page

These notes are for whoever reviews the page. They can be left off a public version.

Ages 4–7. Young children understand feelings and fairness better than causes, and learn by doing small, hands-on things with an adult. That's why the page suggests thanking the action and skipping prizes: material rewards for helping made very young children less likely to help on their own later. (AAP, 2025; Sigelman, 2012; Warneken & Tomasello, 2008)

Ages 8–11. Around this age, children start comparing themselves with classmates and noticing who has what. School studies tie stigma to classmates being able to tell who gets help, which is why the page keeps giving private and celebrates whole classes. (AAP, 2025; Mistry et al., 2015; Orta-Aleman et al., 2024)

Ages 12–13. Young teens want a real say. Service they choose, with a little time to reflect afterward, builds lasting habits, while required service doesn't show the same long-term effect. (Kim & Morgül, 2017; Celio, Durlak & Dymnicki, 2011)

Ages 14 and up. Teens who volunteer by choice are more likely to volunteer as adults, and a shift beside experienced volunteers makes newcomers more likely to come back. (Kim & Morgül, 2017; Tipnis, Chen & Gao, 2026)

Talking with kids. The conversation guidance follows the AAP's age-by-age advice. The 20% figure comes from Women Give 2013, an observational study, so it shows a strong link rather than proof. The grandparent section draws on research linking family stories to children's well-being. (Fivush, Bohanek & Duke, 2010)
